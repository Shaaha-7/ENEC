Executive summary

We keep swarms out of the intake and return them to the sea alive

Problem

Blue Blubber swarms (30–45 cm, 1–3 kg) drift onto seawater intakes. Clearing them takes heavy manual work and almost none survive.

Solution

After ENEC’s alert, a drift planner decides when to act; a bubble curtain at the breakwater gap holds the swarm out; two uncrewed boats tow a boom with a closed-bottom bag and release the jellyfish alive. An AI gap camera is the backup.

Evidence

Simulation on real 2025 Gulf currents and wind, stress-tested against physics the planner does not know; online dashboard; 26 tests; open code.

Next

A 1:20 tank test (AED 300 kit) to measure what the model assumes, then a sea pilot with ENEC.

The problem after the warning

A large, local, hard-to-handle jellyfish

ENEC already detects swarms offshore. The open question is what to do next: manage high volumes with less manual work, less harm and low energy, in line with the UAE Net Zero 2050 and biodiversity goals.

The target: Blue Blubber

  • One of the two dominant outbreak species on the UAE coast; reproduces year-round (Aravind 2024)
  • 30–45 cm bell, ~1–3 kg each (Yang et al. 2018 mass formula)
  • 87–100% sit in the top 1 m by day (Pitt & Kingsford)
  • Large adults swim >10 cm/s: not just passive drifters

What happens at intakes

  • Swarms follow the intake flow through the breakwater opening and pile on screens
  • Worldwide this has forced power reductions and shutdowns (e.g. Torness 2011, Oskarshamn 2013)
  • In the region, ~300 t of jellyfish clogged intakes in Oman

Our solution

Guard the mouth, move the water, keep them alive

1 · The mouth is a chokepoint

At Barakah, seawater reaches the pumps through one ~3 km channel. We measured its mouth on satellite images: 967 m.

2 · Move the water, not the animal

A rising-bubble current across the mouth holds jellyfish out at sea; nothing is cut, pumped or netted.

3 · Proven tools, made autonomous

Oil-spill booms on uncrewed boats, plus a bag that stops leaks. Release west, away from the discharge.

How it works

One swarm, from alert to live release

1
Alert.

ENEC reports a swarm, e.g. 15 km NNW. The planner receives it automatically.

2
Plan.

400 simulated drifts of 60 swimming jellyfish each, on the live current and wind forecast: 44% chance of arrival, window 16–73 h.

3
Prepare.

Curtain on at +13 h; uncrewed boats staged on the approach side; release point chosen.

4
Deflect.

Curtain across the gap holds the swarm outside; airflow follows the current.

5
Gather.

Two uncrewed surface vessels, supervised from shore, close a U-boom with a closed-bottom bag.

6
Release.

Tow ~3 km NNW (≤0.2 m/s through the water: ~7 h over the ground, up to a day against strong inflow) and open the bag; ~60% drift back and are held again.

7
Learn.

An AI camera counts jellyfish at the gap and starts the curtain if they arrive early. Its records are the training data for a learning model that corrects the drift forecast.

Qandeel operator dashboard (working prototype, Streamlit): chance of arrival, window, switch-on clock time, swarm map, curtain status, release point. Data: Copernicus SMOC currents + ERA5 wind, summer 2025.

Evidence 1 · real Gulf data, honest test

Better protection than a camera alone, at a known cost

100 alerts at random times and places in the real 2025 season; the truth uses physics the planner does not know. Base case 1% wind drift. “Arrived” = within 2 km of the gap (15 km NNW alert: 44%; within 500 m: 31%).

3% vs 23% get through

Truth: currents ×0.7–1.3, wind drift ×0.5–1.7, an unmodelled 0–3 cm/s current. Three random seeds: 2.7–3.7% vs 20–26%. A daylight camera misses night arrivals.

The cost

~1,800 curtain hours vs ~700 for camera-only (~12 h, ~USD 500 of energy, per alert); ~80% fewer than running on every alert. In return: 1–2 days of warning.

What this is not

Still simulation against simulation, not real jellyfish. Next: compare drift with real Gulf drifter tracks and ENEC swarm records.

Evidence 2 · curtain and boom

Our finding: oil-spill booms leak jellyfish, and a bag fixes it

400 individually simulated jellyfish per point; surface current from Bulson’s pneumatic-barrier relation.
Jellyfish depth from field data; apex downflow 25% (pessimistic 50%) of tow speed.

Curtain holds, if adults swim slowly

Water approaches the gap at 0.14 m/s on average, 0.23 m/s in the worst 5% of hours (0.20 / 0.36 if wind drift is 3%). 3 L/s per m holds ~0.27 m/s; 4.5 holds ~0.33. Over a full 82 h event: 100% held at 0.2 m/s, but 78% if adults swim 20 cm/s.

Why the bag matters

Oil floats back up; neutrally buoyant jellyfish are dragged under the skirt far below the 0.35 m/s oil-boom limit (0–55% kept at 0.2 m/s). A closed-bottom fabric bag keeps 84–100% in the model.

What we assume, and what we measure first

AssumptionBasisIf wrong

Apex downflow 25–50% of tow speed; bag passes 20%Model hypothesisMeasured in the tank test

Limitations: tested only against simulations; currents from one model point, rounded to ~0.03 m/s by the free API (direct Copernicus download ready); no depth: the curtain draws water in near the bed, so deep or night-time jellyfish could pass under; species at Barakah and adult weight to confirm. Why not harvest? A full bag can go to a licensed processor; release is the default while no UAE market exists.

Sensitivity: approach current, bag leakage / apex downflow and gap width drive the results, so we measure them first.

If shortlisted

From simulation to sea, one measured step at a time

Done

1 · Simulation

Real-data planner, curtain, boom, release and logistics models; online dashboard; stress test; 26 tests.

Weeks 1–2

2 · 1:20 tank test

AED 300 kit: aquarium air line, pump, neutrally buoyant agar mock jellyfish. Measure curtain hold, apex downflow, bag retention.

With ENEC

3 · Sea pilot

One curtain section and one uncrewed boom trial in bloom season; FANR approval; drift checked against tracked swarms; survival and effects on other species.

Then

4 · Scale-up

Full gap; live ENEC alerts into the planner; learning model trained on camera and swarm records corrects the forecast.

Pass marks

  • Tank: ≥95% held at the scaled 0.2 m/s current
  • Tank: bag keeps ≥90% at the scaled 0.2 m/s tow
  • Pilot: no harm to non-target species; fewer screen-clearing hours

Open work and AI use

Live dashboard: enechackathon.streamlit.app. Code, data and tests: github.com/Shaaha-7/ENEC. AI assistants helped search and summarise literature, check claims, draft text and write simulation code, and an open text-to-speech model voices the video; the team reviewed every decision, assumption and figure, and can explain each in our own words.

Approvals we plan for

FANR for any change at the intake; EAD for marine works and moving live animals; Coast Guard and plant security for boats; an engineering safety case for air near the pumps.

What we need from ENEC

Screen-clearing logs and alert history; intake flow rate; bathymetry and the gap’s real width; species ID at Barakah; event frequency.

Beyond the intake

Survey polyps on the breakwater and intake structures, where blooms start. The same kit fits Gulf desalination plants (Taweelah, Fujairah, Oman) and other coastal power stations.
